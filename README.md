## Question

How does household income relate to the share of expenditure devoted to food in Mexico, and does this relationship differ between urban and rural areas? 

This question is relevant for public policy because it identifies which households allocate the largest proportion of their budget to food. If low-income households spend a larger share of their income on food, a Value-Added Tax (VAT) on basic groceries would act as a regressive tax, disproportionately affecting the poorest households and requiring targeted compensation strategies.

## Estimand

The estimand is the semi-elasticity of the household food expenditure share with respect to income per capita (β1), and the difference in this semi-elasticity between rural and urban households (β3). 

In formula terms: 

CEF[Food Share | Income, Rural] = β0 + β1 log(Income) + β2 Rural + β3 [log(Income) × Rural] + γ'Controls

The population is all households in Mexico covered by the ENIGH 2024 sample. This is a descriptive feature of the CEF (Best Linear Predictor), not a causal effect.

## Data

*   **Source:** National Survey of Household Income and Expenditure (ENIGH) 2024, published by INEGI.
*   **Unit of observation:** Household.
*   **Design:** Probabilistic, stratified, two-stage, and cluster sampling. The analysis uses the survey weights (`factor`) and clusters standard errors at the Primary Sampling Unit (UPM) level, nested within survey strata (`est_dis`).
*   **How to obtain it:** The raw CSV file is not included due to GitHub size limits. Detailed instructions to download it manually are provided in `data/get_data.py`.

## Main Findings

*   **Engel's Law holds:** A 1% increase in income per capita is associated with a decrease of approximately 0.09 percentage points in the overall food expenditure share.
*   **Robustness and Regional Differences:** When restricting the food basket to solid items consumed at home, the income effect strengthens (-0.11), and the urban-rural difference becomes statistically significant. Urban households exhibit a more negative income semi-elasticity than rural ones.
*   **Policy Implications (Fiscal Incidence):** A simulated 16% VAT on basic groceries would impose a budget shock of 7.16% on the poorest rural households, compared to just 2.90% on the richest urban households. The tax is systematically more burdensome for rural areas at every income level, justifying the need for targeted transfers.
